Use a First Draft, Critique, Rewrite Loop

A better prompt is often not one perfect instruction, but a small editing loop: draft, critique from a real reader’s view, then rewrite the parts that failed.

Updated June 28, 2026

Start With A Usable First Draft

The easiest way to improve results is to stop asking the AI for the final answer on the first try. Treat the first response as raw material. Ask for a complete draft, but give it a clear job, audience, and format. For example: "Draft a short email to my landlord asking for a repair appointment. Keep it polite, firm, and under 150 words." This gives you something concrete to inspect instead of endlessly adjusting a vague request.

The tradeoff is that the first draft may sound generic, too eager, or slightly off in tone. That is normal. Do not spend ten minutes rewriting your original prompt to prevent every possible mistake. Get the draft on the page, then use the next step to make the AI look at it more critically.

Ask For A Specific Critique

Once you have a draft, do not simply ask, "Is this good?" That usually produces soft praise and minor edits. Instead, ask the AI to critique it from one specific reader’s point of view. A critique means a focused review of what is weak, confusing, risky, or unconvincing. For example: "Critique this as a busy hiring manager who has 30 seconds to read it" or "Critique this as a skeptical client worried about cost."

This works because different readers notice different problems. A busy manager sees buried points. A cautious client sees vague promises. A teacher sees unsupported claims. The more concrete the reader is, the sharper the critique becomes. Avoid fake roles like "world-class expert" unless that role tells the AI what to look for. Better: "a tired parent reading this school trip plan on their phone."

Turn The Critique Into An Editing List

After the critique, resist the urge to say, "Rewrite the whole thing." Full rewrites often fix one issue while damaging parts that were already working. Instead, ask the AI to identify the weakest parts and revise only those. For example: "Based on that critique, list the three weakest sentences, explain why each fails, and rewrite only those sentences. Keep the rest unchanged."

This keeps you in control. You can accept the useful fixes and ignore the ones that do not fit your taste. It also prevents the AI from drifting away from your goal. If the critique says your travel plan is too packed, ask it to rewrite only the schedule section. If your email sounds apologetic, ask it to revise only the opening and request line.

Save The Loop For Next Time

The real habit is the loop, not any single magic phrase. First ask for a draft. Then ask for a pointed critique from the reader who matters most. Then ask for targeted rewrites of the weak spots. This works for emails, resumes, meeting notes, research summaries, party plans, customer messages, and difficult personal writing.

When a loop gives you a result you like, ask the AI to turn the whole exchange into a reusable prompt. For example: "Create a reusable prompt from this process so I can use it next time for similar emails." Save that prompt, but keep the three-step habit. The point is not to make the AI sound impressive. The point is to make the output survive contact with the person who will actually read it.

Key takeaways

  • Do not expect the first AI answer to be final. Use it as a draft to improve.
  • Ask for critique from a specific reader, such as a busy manager, skeptical client, or tired parent.
  • Avoid vague review prompts like "make this better." Tell the AI what kind of failure to look for.
  • Rewrite only the weakest parts so strong sections do not get needlessly changed.
  • Save successful draft, critique, rewrite loops as reusable prompts for future work.
