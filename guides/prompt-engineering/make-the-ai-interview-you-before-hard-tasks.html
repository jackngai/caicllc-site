Make the AI Interview You Before Hard Tasks

For difficult prompts, ask the AI to question you first so it can find the missing context before it writes the thing you actually need.

Updated June 28, 2026

Start With An Interview, Not A Draft

When a request has moving parts, the first prompt should not be “write this for me.” It should be “interview me first.” The central habit is simple: make the AI gather the facts, goals, limits, and preferences before it produces an email, plan, recommendation, or piece of writing.

Use this starter prompt: “I need help with [task]. Before you answer, interview me one question at a time until you have enough context. Ask only the next most important question. When you are ready, summarize what you learned and then draft.” This slows the first minute down, but it usually saves five rounds of fixing a generic answer.

One Question Keeps You Honest

The “one question at a time” part matters because long question lists turn into homework. If the AI asks ten things at once, you will skip details, answer vaguely, or lose patience. One question creates a conversation. It lets the AI adjust based on what you just said.

For example, if you ask for a resignation email, the first question might be, “Do you want to preserve the relationship?” If you say yes, the next question may ask about notice period. If you say no, it may ask how direct the tone should be. The same works for planning a trip, comparing schools, preparing for a hard conversation, or writing a client update.

Make The Missing Pieces Visible

The interview works because hard tasks often fail from hidden assumptions. An assumption is something the AI guesses because you did not say it. It may guess the audience is friendly, the deadline is flexible, the budget is high, or the tone should be polished and warm. Those guesses can be wrong in ways that make the output useless.

Ask the AI to look for four kinds of missing information: goal, audience, constraints, and taste. Goal means what the result must accomplish. Audience means who will read or use it. Constraints are limits like length, budget, deadline, format, or things to avoid. Taste means your preference for tone and style, such as plain, firm, warm, skeptical, brief, or detailed.

Draft Only After A Checkpoint

Before the AI writes the final answer, have it pause and repeat the plan back to you. This checkpoint can be short: “Before drafting, summarize my goal, audience, constraints, and your proposed structure. Wait for my approval.” A structure is just the order of parts. For a proposal, that might be problem, recommendation, cost, risks, next step.

This is where you catch weak direction early. If the plan includes a long introduction for a busy manager, tell it to cut the intro. If the recommendation ignores cost, tell it to add a budget section. If the tone sounds too soft for a complaint email, tell it to be clearer. Then ask for the draft.

Save The Good Version

Once the interview produces a strong result, do not throw the conversation away. Ask: “Turn this whole process into a reusable prompt I can use next time.” This gives you a template for future emails, research requests, meeting plans, or writing projects.

The habit is not to make every prompt longer. It is to make the beginning smarter. For easy tasks, a direct prompt is fine. For hard tasks, let the AI interview you first, confirm the plan, then write. Better answers usually come from better questions before the draft exists.

Key takeaways

  • For complex requests, start with “interview me one question at a time” instead of asking for an immediate draft.
  • One question at a time prevents overwhelm and helps the AI adapt to each answer.
  • Have the AI uncover goal, audience, constraints, and taste before it writes.
  • Use a checkpoint where the AI summarizes the plan before drafting.
  • After a strong result, ask the AI to turn the process into a reusable prompt.
