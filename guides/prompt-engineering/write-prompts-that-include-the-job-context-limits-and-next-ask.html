Write Prompts That Include the Job, Context, Limits, and Next Ask

A good prompt is not a clever script, but a simple handoff: name the job, give the situation, set the boundaries, then ask what is needed next.

Updated July 19, 2026

Start With The Job

The fastest way to improve a prompt is to stop asking the AI to “help” and start giving it a job. “Help me with this email” is too vague. “Act as a careful editor and rewrite this email so it is clear, polite, and under 150 words” gives the chat box a role and a result.

This does not mean pretending the AI is a person with a fancy title. It means naming the work you want done. Use everyday job labels: summarize, compare, draft, plan, critique, organize, decide. If the output is weak, often the job was fuzzy. Instead of “What should I do about my trip?” try “Build a two-day itinerary for a first-time visitor who wants museums, good food, and no rushed mornings.”

Add The Context

Once the job is clear, give the background the AI cannot guess. Context means the facts, preferences, audience, and situation that change what a good answer looks like. For an email, that may include who will read it, your relationship, the tone you want, and what happened before. For research, it may include what you already know and what decision you are trying to make.

The common failure here is either too little context or a messy paste with no signal. Give the few details that matter most. “I’m writing to my manager after missing a deadline. I want to take responsibility without over-explaining. The project is now ready.” That is enough for a much better draft. You can always add more later.

Set The Limits

After context, add limits so the answer fits your real use. Limits are simple boundaries: length, tone, format, sources, budget, time, reading level, or things to avoid. They protect you from answers that are technically fine but useless, like a 900-word email when you needed three sentences.

Good limits are specific but not suffocating. “Give me three options” is useful. “Make it warm, direct, and not apologetic” is useful. “Plan dinners under $30 per person within a 15-minute walk” is useful. Too many limits can make the answer brittle, so start with the constraints that would actually make you reject the result.

End With The Next Ask

The last part is the most overlooked: ask the AI to tell you what it still needs. A strong closing line is, “What do you need from me to give your best answer?” This turns the prompt into a short interview instead of a guessing game. If the task matters, let the AI ask clarifying questions before it writes.

For bigger tasks, do not ask for everything at once. Break the work into checkpoints: first gather questions, then make an outline, then draft, then revise. For high-stakes facts, verify before trusting. Compare important claims against other reliable sources or another AI model, and look for agreement. The four-part shape stays the same: job, context, limits, next ask. It is simple enough to remember, and strong enough to use every day.

Key takeaways

  • Name the job first: draft, summarize, compare, plan, critique, organize, or decide.
  • Give only the context that changes what a good answer would be.
  • Use limits like length, tone, format, budget, audience, and things to avoid.
  • End important prompts with: “What do you need from me to give your best answer?”
  • Break larger requests into checkpoints instead of asking for one giant answer.
  • Verify high-stakes facts with other reliable sources before acting on them.
