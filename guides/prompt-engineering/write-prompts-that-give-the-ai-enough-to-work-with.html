Write Prompts That Give the AI Enough to Work With

Better AI answers come from giving the chat box four things in order: the task, the background, the limits, and the kind of response you want.

Updated July 12, 2026

Start With The Job

A weak prompt makes the AI guess what you mean. A strong prompt gives it a job. Start by saying the task in plain words: “Rewrite this email so it sounds firm but polite,” “Help me plan a three-day visit to Chicago,” or “Summarize this article for someone who has five minutes.”

This matters because the same information can be used in different ways. If you paste a messy paragraph and write “Thoughts?”, you may get advice, a rewrite, a summary, or a lecture. Instead, lead with the action you want. Use a verb: write, compare, shorten, plan, explain, list, draft, critique, or prepare.

Add The Missing Context

Once the job is clear, add the background the AI cannot see. Context means the details that would help a smart assistant understand the situation: who the answer is for, what has already happened, what tone fits, what you care about, and what would count as success.

For example, do not write, “Help me respond to this client.” Write, “Help me respond to a long-time client who is upset about a delayed invoice. I want to keep the relationship, take responsibility without over-apologizing, and offer a clear next step.” The tradeoff is that context takes an extra minute. The payoff is fewer bland answers and fewer rounds of correction.

Set Useful Limits

After context, add limits. Limits are not there to make the AI less helpful. They keep the answer inside the shape you need. Useful limits include length, tone, reading level, time, budget, audience, format, and what to avoid.

A planning prompt might say, “Keep activities under $50 per person, avoid late nights, include one indoor backup for rain, and assume we will not rent a car.” A writing prompt might say, “Use warm but direct language, keep it under 150 words, and do not sound salesy.” Without limits, the AI often gives the average answer: too long, too broad, and not quite usable.

Ask For The Exact Output

Now finish by saying what kind of answer you want. This is the fourth part: the ask. If you want options, ask for three options. If you want a finished draft, ask for a finished draft. If you want help thinking, ask for questions first. A simple line works well: “Before answering, ask me what you need to know to give your best answer.”

Put the four parts together like this: “Task: Draft a reply to my manager. Background: I need to ask for Friday off because of a family appointment, but our team is busy and I want to sound responsible. Limits: Keep it under 120 words, professional, and do not over-explain. Ask: Give me two versions, one warm and one more direct.” This structure does not make prompting complicated. It removes the guessing. The AI can only work with what you give it, so give it the job, the situation, the boundaries, and the finish line.

Key takeaways

  • Use a four-part prompt: task, background, limits, and exact ask.
  • Start with a clear verb such as draft, compare, shorten, plan, or explain.
  • Add the real-world details the AI cannot infer from the chat box.
  • Set limits for length, tone, budget, audience, time, and what to avoid.
  • When unsure, add: “What do you need from me to give your best answer?”
