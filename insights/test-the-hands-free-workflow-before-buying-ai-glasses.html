Test the Hands-Free Workflow Before Buying AI Glasses

The useful business question is where seeing the task could save an employee from stopping to explain it.

November 24, 2026

  1. Find one costly interruption

    Choose a low-risk task where someone repeatedly stops working to consult instructions, such as packing an order or locating a supply. Count those interruptions and time the task today. That gives your wearable AI pilot a specific problem to solve.

  2. Prototype with a phone

    Use a mounted phone with a camera-and-voice assistant to test whether visual context helps before buying wearable hardware. Supply your approved checklist and ask: “Guide me through this checklist one step at a time. Ask me to confirm each step, and request a closer view when something is unclear.” This tests the workflow, though it will not establish glasses performance.

  3. Keep observation separate from verification

    Ask the assistant to describe what it sees before suggesting the next action. Have the operator verify quantities, labels, and completed steps against the checklist. Measure errors and corrections alongside time saved, so a faster interaction does not hide extra rework.

  4. Make consent part of the pilot

    Start in a controlled workspace with participating staff and keep customers, private paperwork, and unrelated screens out of view. Explain what the camera captures and where that information goes. Recording lights and face redaction do not establish permission from people nearby.

Why it matters

A polished demonstration does not establish whether wearable AI improves your work. A narrow pilot gives a small business evidence about time saved, mistakes introduced, and staff acceptance before committing to hardware.
